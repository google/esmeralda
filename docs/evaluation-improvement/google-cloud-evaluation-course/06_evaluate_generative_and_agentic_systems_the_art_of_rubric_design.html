In this lesson, you'll learn how to design, write, and iteratively refine rubrics to evaluate agentic and generative systems.

Converting subjective user vibes into clear, repeatable metrics is one of the biggest causes of failed agentic and generative system development efforts. In this lesson, you'll learn how to write precise operational definitions, how to balance rubric detail against inflexibility, the steps to make rubrics stronger with human agreement, and walk through improvement of a rubric (including an example).

Rubrics as the foundation of evaluation

Business outcomes

When you evaluate a generative or agentic system, you must have a way to measure quality that goes beyond subjective "vibes." Informal reviews are useful during early prototyping, but they are too inconsistent to guide systemic hill climbing.

To perform useful evaluation of subjective tasks, you must establish a rubric. In agentic and generative systems, a rubric is a set of guidelines. It defines a high-quality output when many valid outputs exist.

Rubrics are the foundation for both human computation and automated evaluation of tasks lacking straightforward ground truth:

Without a strong and clear rubric, you cannot trust your evaluation. If your human annotators do not have a shared definition of quality, your autoraters will not either. A weak rubric produces random, unhelpful evaluations. These mislead your analysis, ruin hill climbing, and derail your launch.

Moving from vague criteria to operational definitions

Business outcomes

To build an effective rubric, you must move away from vague quality descriptions and establish operational definitions. An operational definition is a clear, unambiguous definition of a quality attribute in measurable terms. If five people grade an output using an operational definition, they must all arrive at the same rating.

For example, instructing an annotator or an autorater to check if a chatbot response "sounds professional" is highly subjective.

Instead, you must operationalize "professional" into concrete criteria:

  • The chatbot does not apologize in the first sentence.

  • The chatbot uses an active voice.

  • The chatbot directs the user to a clear next step.

Completeness

Response is incomplete or misses the main point.

Response is accurate but could be more comprehensive.

Conciseness & relevance

Response is overly verbose, contains irrelevant information, or is confusingly short.

Tone & empathy

Tone is inappropriate, dismissive, or unhelpful.

Tone is neutral and professional but lacks any sense of empathy.

Tone is professional, polite, and shows appropriate empathy for the user's situation.

The centralized gateway architecture

Above, we see that even a carefully rewritten rubric can still be improved. No matter how much you work on a rubric, you can improve it. But eventually you might run into difficulty knowing if you have the right length, exhaustiveness, and specificity.

The way to know your rubric is good is to iteratively refine it. Do not expect to write a perfect rubric on your first draft. Instead, iterate through a structured feedback loop:

Draft a pilot rubric
Annotate a small test batch
Analyze disagreements
Refine the criteria
Iterate

Rubric iteration requires a deep understanding of system requirements and edge cases. For this reason, the development team, product managers, and subject matter experts (SMEs) must lead the process.

Do not delegate initial rubric design to generic, crowdsourced annotator pools. They lack the context needed to identify and resolve subtle rubric ambiguities.

The centralized gateway architecture

To verify that your rubric is objective, you must measure annotator agreement. Ensure you test annotators who did not build the rubric. This naive agreement is the ultimate metric of rubric strength. If different annotators use a rubric and arrive at similar ratings, the rubric is strong.

When you measure agreement, do not rely only on simple percentage agreement.

The centralized gateway architecture

Percent agreement is highly misleading. It does not account for chance agreement, and it biases outcomes towards the middle of the scale.
