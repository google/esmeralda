Use Model Hill Climbing for ADK Agent Optimization

The 8-step hill climbing loop

Source: https://storage.googleapis.com/cloud-training/cls-html5-courses/P-UMHHZD-A/content/index.html#/lessons/OSFOsBnXCvRY-7Wd3-zm9TM7RdOuRsW6
In this lesson, you'll learn the eight-step loop that turns hill climbing from an idea into a repeatable process. The first two steps set you up. The next six are the loop you run over and over until the system is good enough or you decide to stop.

You'll start with the two pre-climb steps: formalizing your evaluation and objectives, then establishing a baseline. From there, you'll walk the repeating cycle: analyze failures, form hypotheses, prioritize, make one change, measure, and iterate. Throughout, you'll see why small changes and fast iteration matter more than big, batched edits.
Pre-hill climbing: Objectives and baselines
The full loop has eight steps: the first two are setup, and the last six repeat.

Step 1: Formalize your evaluation and objectives

You establish an evaluation plan and a clear definition of "good enough," tied to organizational priorities. This is where you decide which metrics matter and how much. Quality rarely stands alone, so fold cost and latency directly into your objectives, potentially combining them with output quality metrics into a single score to hill climb. A common form subtracts weighted penalties for cost and latency from quality:

composite_score = quality - (weight_cost * cost) - (weight_latency * latency)

Set the weights once, with the project sponsor, and fix the target before you start. A target you renegotiate mid-climb to fit a result you already like isn't a target.

Step 2: Establish a baseline

You run the evaluation against the current system and record its state: output quality, cost, latency, and any other metrics that matter. This baseline is the reference every later change is measured against. Without the baseline, you cannot tell whether a change helped, hurt, or did nothing.

Do not skip establishing a baseline. A wrong or incomplete baseline corrupts every downstream decision. If your baseline misses an important metric, you might improve tracked metrics while quietly breaking untracked ones.

The hill climbing loop

Step 3: Analyze failure patterns

With objectives and a baseline in place, you enter the main hill climbing loop.

You interpret your evaluation metrics and analyze trajectories to find where the system fails. A trajectory is the step-by-step record of what the system did to reach its final output. The goal here is to locate the failures, not yet to fix them.

Step 5: Prioritize hypotheses.

You can't test everything at once. Weigh each candidate hypothesis on three factors: effort and time to test the hypothesis, likelihood of success, and value of the predicted improvement. Prioritize cheap and easy experiments with high expected payoffs.

Step 6: Implement the best hypothesis.

Make the smallest change that tests the idea, and change only that one thing. If you rewrite the prompt and swap the model and the score moves, you've learned nothing about which edit did it. One change to the system at a time is the only way you can attribute changes in metrics to the change in the system.

Step 7: Take measurements and compare.

You run some or all of the evaluation plan and compare the result against your baseline and your previous iterations.

Don't forget that generative model non-determinism means a single run can mislead you. Evaluation of iterations needs some repetition so you do not judge a change against run-to-run noise. If the change clearly rises above the run-to-run noise, it becomes your new baseline. If it doesn't you revert.

Step 8: Iterate.

You return to step 3 and repeat, usually with the next hypothesis on your list. Sometimes you revisit your objectives instead, if the loop revealed that you were measuring the wrong thing. Above all, protect your speed of iteration. Fast loops let you conduct more experiments. Favor small, quick experiments over slow, exhaustive ones.
