In this lesson, you'll learn to diagnose failures by reading the path a system takes to its final outcome, not just the outcome itself. That path is the trajectory, and it's your primary diagnostic tool.

You'll begin by differentiating a trajectory from a trace, and outcome evaluation from trajectory evaluation.

Next, you'll use root cause analysis to locate the first uncorrected failure in a trajectory, and classify this failure using a standard taxonomy.

You'll then evaluate trajectory paths using ground truth paths and identify when their rigidity creates false negatives. Finally, you'll prioritize what trajectories to analyze, and scale trajectory analysis from one to thousands.

The trajectory as your primary diagnostic

A trajectory is the ordered sequence of events between a system input and its final output. It records the system's reasoning steps, tool calls, observations, state changes, and any handoffs or interventions.

People use "trajectory" and "trace" interchangeably, but the distinction is useful to understand. A trajectory is the conceptual path: the steps, thoughts, and actions you reconstruct to follow the process.

Most production failures happen inside the trajectory, not in the final answer. That's why the trajectory is your primary diagnostic tool. For a multi-step agent, rating only the final answer tells you whether it succeeded, but not why it failed. A wrong answer arrives with no explanation of which step broke. The failure could occur in the first tool call, the fifth reasoning step, or anywhere in between.

Root cause analysis and failure classification

When a trajectory fails, you need to find where it went wrong, not just that it went wrong. Root cause analysis (RCA) isolates the initial point of failure.

This matters because later steps are contaminated by earlier malfunctions. Once a trajectory goes off track, every following step reasons about a broken state. Fixing a late symptom won't help if the real break happened five steps earlier.

To perform RCA, read the trajectory chronologically from top to bottom and isolate the steps that went off track. Then find the first off-track step the agent could not self-correct from. That step is the root cause.

Self-correction makes this analysis tricky. Agents often attempt to recover from errors. They might make a mistake, notice the error, and fix it in the next step. In this case, the first failed step is not the root cause because the agent resolved it.

The root cause is a later failed step, the one the agent could not not recover from. You are not looking for the first error; you are looking for the first error that stuck.

Long-horizon tasks produce long trajectories, making manual RCA difficult. Reading hundreds of steps is slow and error-prone. For these systems, use specialized trajectory analysis tools or prompt a foundation model to analyze the trajectory and locate the failure step(s).

Ground truth trajectories and golden paths

A ground truth trajectory, or golden path, is a predefined, step-by-step reference sequence of the actions, thoughts, and tool calls you expect for a specific input. Ground truth trajectories can be very trustworthy when they are created and verified by human annotators.

Analyzing successful trajectories is critical before scaling your system. Inefficiencies like redundant tool calls or bloated reasoning generate unnecessary costs and latency on every run.

While negligible at low volumes, this waste compounds at scale. Fixing these issues early is far more cost-effective than patching them in production.

Holistic trajectory analysis and metrics

Individual RCA is essential for debugging specific failures. However, improving overall system performance requires aggregate metrics to capture patterns across hundreds or thousands of runs.

While one trajectory explains a single failure, aggregate metrics identify systemic issues.

Model-based trajectory metrics use autoraters to assess trajectory characteristics such as reasoning coherence and tool selection quality across diverse trajectories at scale. Autoraters with adaptive rubrics are especially suited for this.

Make sure you calibrate your approach for model-based trajectory metrics against human judgment to ensure its ratings are reliable.

Common model-based trajectory analysis techniques include:

  1. 1. Failure categorization: Using autoraters to sort failed trajectories into predefined taxonomy buckets.
  2. 2. Automated issue clustering: Grouping similar failed trajectories to identify systemic issues.

The payoff is prioritization. Categorization can uncover problematic tools, subagents, and other dependencies causing a wide variety of errors.

Clustering can reveal that seemingly unrelated failures share a common root cause, revealing especially impactful hill climbing opportunities that might otherwise go missed.
